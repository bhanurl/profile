“Crack”: Race, National Security & Domestic Criminalization

Michael Javen Fortner, Bhanu Cheepurupalli & Aria Fafat

Early in the Reagan administration, the politics of drugs and the politics of “illegal immigrants” were treated as two sides of the same coin. This paper traces how 1980s drug enforcement fused race, immigration status, and the language of national security — and how that fusion reshaped who got policed, and how.

Harlem, one witness told a House narcotics committee, was “under siege” — locked in a “battle” over who would control the community.

Drawing on congressional hearings, DEA reports, and the correspondence of figures like Rep. Charlie Rangel, we follow the paper trail: from Miami Chamber of Commerce memos urging that the Coast Guard be redirected toward “drugs and illegal aliens,” to calls for the INS to verify the status of those arrested on narcotics charges and “promptly deport” them.

What emerges is a domestic-criminalization regime in which the “crack” panic and immigration enforcement were built together — a history whose shadows fall across today's debates over policing, security, and belonging.
